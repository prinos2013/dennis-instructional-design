Topic 6: Writing Learning Objectives

Week 6 | Bloom's Revised Taxonomy, Mager's approach, ABCD model, alignment with assessment

Overview

A learning objective says exactly what the learner will be able to do after instruction. Objectives are like a destination in a journey: without them, you cannot choose a route (activities) or know when you have arrived (assessment).

Poorly written objectives such as "Students will understand networking" are vague. Strong objectives such as "Students will be able to configure a home Wi-Fi router using the manufacturer's manual" can be observed and measured.

1. Goals vs. Objectives

Goal Objective
Level Broad, general Specific, narrow
Time Long term (course or programme) Short term (lesson or unit)
Measurable? Not directly Yes
Example Learners will use ICT effectively in teaching Learners will create a 5-slide presentation using PowerPoint with at least two images

2. Why Objectives Matter

  • Guide the selection of content, strategies and media.
  • Tell learners what is expected.
  • Make assessment fair and focused.
  • Allow evaluation of instruction.

3. Mager's Approach (Robert Mager)

Mager said a good objective has three components:

Component Meaning Example
Performance (behaviour) What the learner will do (an action verb) create a spreadsheet
Condition Under what circumstances; what tools/help are given using Microsoft Excel and a sample dataset
Criterion (standard) How well, how fast, how accurately with no formula errors within 20 minutes
Example: "Given a sample dataset and Microsoft Excel (condition), the learner will create a summary table (performance) with no formula errors within 20 minutes (criterion)."

4. The ABCD Model

ABCD (from Heinich, Molenda and colleagues) is similar to Mager but adds the Audience:

Element Letter Question answered
AudienceAWho is the learner?
BehaviourBWhat will they do? (observable action verb)
ConditionCUnder what conditions?
DegreeDHow well? (standard)
Example: A: Form 3 students B: will identify the parts of a computer C: from a labelled diagram D: with at least 8 out of 10 correct.
Key Point: Mager = Performance, Condition, Criterion. ABCD = Audience, Behaviour, Condition, Degree. They are the same idea; ABCD simply names the audience.

5. Bloom's Taxonomy (Revised, Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001)

Bloom's Taxonomy classifies learning into levels of thinking, from simple to complex. The revised version uses action verbs for each level instead of nouns:

Level Meaning Sample verbs Example objective
1. Remember Recall facts define, list, name, state, recall List four parts of the CPU
2. Understand Explain ideas explain, describe, summarise, classify Explain how a LAN works
3. Apply Use in new situations use, demonstrate, solve, carry out Use a LMS to upload a quiz
4. Analyse Break into parts; find relationships compare, differentiate, examine, organise Compare ADDIE and SAM
5. Evaluate Judge using criteria justify, critique, assess, defend Justify the choice of a model for a project
6. Create Put parts together to make something new design, develop, construct, produce Design an online learning module
  • The original taxonomy used nouns (Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation). In the revision, Synthesis becomes Create and is placed at the top, above Evaluate.
  • The revision also separates knowledge dimensions: factual, conceptual, procedural and metacognitive knowledge.
  • Lower-order thinking skills: remember, understand. Higher-order thinking skills: analyse, evaluate, create.

Verbs to avoid (not observable): know, understand, appreciate, be aware of, learn, be familiar with. These describe internal states you cannot see. Replace them with observable verbs such as "explain", "list", "demonstrate".

6. Other Domains of Learning

Domain Concerned with Example verbs
CognitiveKnowledge and thinkingexplain, solve, design
AffectiveAttitudes, values, feelingschoose, defend, value, accept
PsychomotorPhysical and manual skillsassemble, type, operate, connect

7. Aligning Objectives with Assessment and Activities

Alignment means objectives, assessments and learning activities all match.

Objective Matching assessment Matching activity
Define key ID termsShort quiz or glossary testReading, flashcards
Compare two ID modelsEssay or comparison tableGroup discussion, case study
Design an online moduleProject with rubricHands-on workshop, peer review
Key Point: If an objective says "design" but the exam only asks learners to "list", the assessment is not aligned. The verb in the objective decides the type of assessment.

8. Checklist for a Good Objective

  • Learner-centred: describes what the learner will do, not the teacher.
  • Uses an observable action verb.
  • States conditions where necessary.
  • Gives a standard of performance.
  • Contains one main outcome (not several mixed together).
  • Is realistic and relevant.

Topic 7: Designing Assessments

Week 7 | Formative and summative assessment, authentic assessment, rubrics, alignment

Overview

Assessment answers the question: "How do we know learners have learned what we intended?" In instructional design, assessment is planned before activities are created. This approach is called backward design (Wiggins and McTighe, 2005): first decide the desired results, then decide the evidence, then plan learning activities.

Do not confuse assessment with evaluation. Assessment measures learner performance. Evaluation judges the quality of the instruction (see Topic 13).

1. Formative vs. Summative Assessment

Formative Summative
Purpose To improve learning (assessment FOR learning) To measure achievement at the end (assessment OF learning)
When During instruction At the end of a unit, course or programme
Stakes Low or no marks High marks; grades and certificates
Feedback Immediate and detailed Often a final grade
Examples Quick quizzes, polls, discussion posts, drafts, exit tickets, self-checks End-of-semester exam, final project, certification test
Example: In a Moodle course, a short non-graded quiz after each lesson (formative) helps learners check understanding. The final proctored exam at the end (summative) decides the grade.
  • Diagnostic assessment: before instruction, to find existing knowledge (pre-test).
  • Ipsative assessment: comparing a learner with their own past performance.

2. Types of Test Items

Type Examples Strength Weakness
Objective items Multiple choice, true/false, matching, fill-in-the-blank Fast to mark, reliable, easy to automate online Can test only recall; guessing is possible
Subjective items Essay, short answer, case analysis Test higher-order thinking and writing Slow to mark; marker bias possible
Performance items Practical tasks, presentations, projects, demonstrations Test real skills Time-consuming; need clear rubrics

Tips for writing good multiple-choice questions

  • Write a clear question (stem) with one best answer.
  • Make all options similar in length and believable (good distractors).
  • Avoid "all of the above" and "none of the above" where possible.
  • Avoid negative wording.
  • Test understanding and application, not only memory.

3. Authentic Assessment

Definition: Authentic assessment asks learners to perform real-world tasks that show meaningful application of knowledge and skills.
  • Tasks are realistic, complex and meaningful.
  • Learners produce a product or performance.
  • Standards are clear and criteria are known in advance.
  • Often includes reflection, peer feedback and portfolios.
Traditional Authentic
Write an essay on what a lesson plan isCreate and teach a real lesson plan, and reflect on it
Multiple-choice test on networking termsSet up a small working network and document it
List parts of a websiteBuild a simple website for a local business

4. Designing Rubrics

Definition: A rubric is a scoring guide that lists criteria and describes levels of performance for each criterion.
  • Analytic rubric: scores each criterion separately (more detailed feedback).
  • Holistic rubric: gives one overall score (faster, less detail).

Sample analytic rubric for an e-learning module

Criterion Excellent (4) Good (3) Fair (2) Weak (1)
ObjectivesClear, measurable, alignedMostly measurableSome vagueMissing or unclear
Content qualityAccurate, organisedMostly accurateSome errorsMany errors
InteractivityHighly engagingSome interactionMinimalNone
AccessibilityFully accessibleMostly accessiblePartlyNot accessible

Benefits: clear expectations, fair marking, useful feedback, supports self- and peer assessment.

Steps: choose criteria linked to objectives, decide performance levels, write clear descriptors, weight the criteria, test the rubric and share it with learners before the task.

5. Quality Characteristics of Good Assessments

Quality Meaning Example
ValidityMeasures what it is supposed to measureA practical task to test typing speed is valid; a theory test is not
ReliabilityGives consistent resultsTwo markers give similar marks using the same rubric
FairnessFree from bias; same chance for all learnersAccessible format for learners with visual impairment
PracticalityFeasible in time, cost and resourcesAuto-marked online quiz for 500 learners
AuthenticityResembles real tasksCreate a real lesson plan
AlignmentMatches objectives and activitiesObjective "design" is assessed with a design task

6. Alignment: The Triangle of Instruction

Good instruction aligns three things: objectives, assessments and learning activities (constructive alignment, Biggs). If one is changed, the others must be checked.

Key Point: The objective verb decides the assessment. "Explain" is assessed with explanation; "Demonstrate" is assessed with demonstration; "Create" is assessed with a product and rubric.

Assessment in online learning

  • Use varied, low-stakes quizzes, discussions, projects and e-portfolios.
  • Use question banks, randomisation and time limits to reduce cheating.
  • Prefer authentic tasks and open-book questions that need application.
  • Give timely feedback; use automated feedback where helpful.
  • Consider internet access and accessibility.

Topic 8: Instructional Strategies and Media Selection

Week 8 | Gagné's Nine Events, Merrill's First Principles, media selection, ASSURE model

Overview

After deciding what learners must achieve (objectives) and how to check it (assessment), the designer decides how to teach it. This is the choice of instructional strategies (the plan and methods) and media (the tools and channels that carry the message).

This topic introduces two major frameworks for planning instruction (Gagné's Nine Events and Merrill's First Principles), and a practical planning model for media use, the ASSURE model.

1. Gagné's Nine Events of Instruction

Robert Gagné linked the way we teach to the internal mental processes of learning (cognitivist). The nine events form a checklist for a complete lesson.

No. Event Purpose Example in a lesson on "Using a spreadsheet"
1Gain attentionArouse interestShow a messy paper record versus a clean spreadsheet
2Inform learners of objectivesSet expectations"By the end you will create a budget with formulas"
3Stimulate recall of prior learningLink to what they knowAsk: "How do you keep a record of money now?"
4Present the contentProvide new informationDemonstrate cells, rows, columns and formulas
5Provide learning guidanceHelp understandingWorked examples, tips, step-by-step guide
6Elicit performance (practice)Let learners tryLearners create a simple budget
7Provide feedbackCorrect and encourageTeacher or peer comments on errors
8Assess performanceCheck achievementA short practical test
9Enhance retention and transferMake learning lastApply to a real task, such as the class fund
Key Point: Memory aid for the order: Attention -> Objectives -> Recall -> Content -> Guidance -> Practice -> Feedback -> Assessment -> Transfer.

2. Merrill's First Principles of Instruction

M. David Merrill reviewed many theories and found five principles common to effective instruction. Learning is promoted when:

Principle Meaning
1. Problem-centred (task-centred)Learners work on real-world problems or tasks
2. ActivationExisting knowledge is activated as a foundation for new learning
3. DemonstrationNew knowledge is demonstrated to the learner (not just told)
4. ApplicationLearners apply new knowledge, with feedback
5. IntegrationLearners integrate new knowledge into everyday life through reflection, discussion or presentation
Example: A course on web design begins with the task "Build a website for a local shop" (problem), reviews learners' prior experience with documents (activation), demonstrates HTML basics (demonstration), lets learners build pages with feedback (application), and has them present their sites to the shop owner (integration).

3. Common Instructional Strategies

Strategy Description Suits
Direct instruction / lectureTeacher presents information clearlyBasic facts, large groups
DemonstrationShow how something is doneProcedures, software skills
DiscussionLearners exchange ideasConcepts, opinions
Cooperative / collaborative learningGroups work towards a common goalComplex tasks, social skills
Problem-based learning (PBL)Learners solve an open-ended problemReal-world thinking
Project-based learningLearners produce a product over timeApplication and creation
Case studyAnalysis of real situationsAnalysis and judgement
Simulation and gamesSafe imitation of real situationsRisky or costly practice
Flipped classroomContent at home (video), practice in classActive class time
Self-paced / individualisedLearners move at their own speedMixed ability, online
  • Teacher-centred strategies: lecture, demonstration.
  • Learner-centred strategies: PBL, projects, discussion, collaboration.
  • Choose according to the objective, learners, context and resources.

4. Selecting Media and Technology

Media are the carriers of instructional messages: print, audio, video, graphics, computers, mobile devices, the internet. Media should serve the objective; they should never be chosen only because they are fashionable.

Criteria for choosing media

  • Alignment with objectives and learners.
  • Accessibility and availability (do learners have devices, power and data?).
  • Cost (purchase, development, maintenance).
  • Technical quality and ease of use.
  • Instructor and learner skill to use it.
  • Interactivity and engagement.
  • Time to produce.

Matching media to purpose

Purpose Suitable media
Show a process or motion (e.g., how data moves in a network)Video, animation, simulation
Teach procedures in softwareScreencast tutorial
Discussion and collaborationForums, WhatsApp groups, video conferencing
Practice and feedbackOnline quizzes, interactive H5P
Reference material for low-bandwidth learnersPDF notes, printed handouts, audio
Key Point: Technology does not by itself improve learning. The strategy matters more than the medium. A simple tool used with a sound strategy beats an expensive tool used poorly.

5. The ASSURE Model

ASSURE (Heinich, Molenda, Russell, Smaldino) is a practical six-step model for planning lessons that integrate media and technology.

Letter Step What you do
AAnalyse learnersStudy characteristics, prior knowledge and learning preferences
SState standards and objectivesWrite measurable objectives (ABCD) linked to standards
SSelect strategies, technology, media and materialsChoose, modify or design resources
UUtilise technology, media and materialsPreview, prepare environment and learners, present
RRequire learner participationActive practice with feedback
EEvaluate and reviseAssess learner achievement and the lesson, then improve
Example: A teacher planning a lesson on "Parts of a computer": analyses the Basic 8 class; states the objective; selects a video and real hardware; previews the video and prepares the lab; makes pupils label parts in groups; evaluates with a quiz and revises the lesson.
