Topic 13: Evaluation of Instruction

Week 13 | Formative and summative evaluation, Kirkpatrick's four levels, quality rubrics (QM, OSCQR)

Overview

Evaluation answers the question: "Did our instruction work, and how can we make it better?" It is the E in ADDIE. Remember the difference: assessment measures what learners have learned; evaluation judges the quality and effectiveness of the instruction itself (the course, programme or materials).

Evaluation is not a single event at the end. It is a continuing process that supports improvement and decision-making.

1. Formative vs. Summative Evaluation

Formative evaluation Summative evaluation
Purpose To improve the instruction while it is being developed or delivered To judge the overall worth or effectiveness of finished instruction
When During design, development and pilot After implementation
Who uses it Designers and developers Decision-makers, managers, funders
Questions What is confusing? What should be changed? Did learners achieve the goals? Is it worth continuing?
Methods Expert review, one-to-one trials, small-group trials, field trials Outcome data, surveys, cost-benefit analysis, impact studies

Dick and Carey's stages of formative evaluation

  • One-to-one trials: the designer works with individual learners to find obvious problems.
  • Small-group trials: 8-20 learners test the materials to check effectiveness and procedures.
  • Field trial: the instruction is tested in a realistic setting with a full class.
  • Expert review: subject-matter, design and technical experts check the materials.
  • Confirmative evaluation (Kemp): done after some time to check whether the instruction remains effective and relevant.

2. Kirkpatrick's Four Levels of Evaluation

Donald Kirkpatrick created this very popular model for evaluating training. It was updated by James and Wendy Kirkpatrick (2016) as the "New World" model.

Level Name Question Typical methods Example
1 Reaction Did learners like it and find it relevant? Feedback forms, surveys "Was the course useful?" rating
2 Learning Did they gain knowledge, skills or attitudes? Pre-test and post-test, practical tasks Quiz scores improved from 40% to 80%
3 Behaviour Are they applying what they learned on the job? Observation, interviews, supervisor reports Teachers now use Moodle weekly
4 Results Did it produce the intended organisational outcomes? Performance data, productivity, cost-benefit Improved student results; reduced paper costs
Key Point: Memory aid: Reaction -> Learning -> Behaviour -> Results. Higher levels are harder and take longer to measure, but give more valuable information.
  • The "New World" model emphasises starting with Level 4 (the desired results) and working backwards, and identifying leading indicators and required drivers (such as coaching and reinforcement) that support transfer of learning.
  • Some authors add a fifth level, ROI (return on investment), by Phillips: comparing benefits with costs.
Example: A bank trains staff to use a new app. L1: staff say the training was clear. L2: test scores rise. L3: staff actually use the app correctly with customers. L4: customer waiting time drops and complaints reduce.

3. Evaluating Online Courses with Quality Rubrics

A quality rubric is a checklist or standards document used to review the design of an online course.

Quality Matters (QM)

  • A widely used, peer-review, research-based quality assurance programme for online and blended courses.
  • The Higher Education Rubric has eight general standards: (1) Course Overview and Introduction; (2) Learning Objectives (Competencies); (3) Assessment and Measurement; (4) Instructional Materials; (5) Learning Activities and Learner Interaction; (6) Course Technology; (7) Learner Support; (8) Accessibility and Usability.
  • Key idea: alignment of objectives, assessments, materials, activities and technology.
  • Courses are reviewed by trained peer reviewers and must meet essential standards.

OSCQR

  • OSCQR = Open SUNY Course Quality Review rubric (from the State University of New York).
  • Free and open; used as a self-review and improvement tool, with a rubric of about 50 standards.
  • Areas include course overview, course information, course technology and tools, design and layout, content and activities, interaction, assessment and feedback, and accessibility.
Quality Matters (QM) OSCQR
CostSubscription / paid review servicesFree, open
Review styleFormal peer review, certification possibleSelf-review and improvement
FocusAlignment and quality standardsPractical checklist for improving courses

4. Evaluation Methods and Data Sources

  • Learner data: test scores, completion rates, assignment results, LMS analytics (logins, time on task).
  • Surveys and questionnaires (Likert scale).
  • Interviews and focus groups.
  • Observation of classes or learners using materials.
  • Expert and peer reviews.
  • Documents: reports, comments, discussion posts.

Basic evaluation steps:

  1. Step 1: Define the purpose and evaluation questions.
  2. Step 2: Choose criteria and indicators.
  3. Step 3: Choose methods and tools.
  4. Step 4: Collect data.
  5. Step 5: Analyse the data.
  6. Step 6: Report findings and make recommendations.
  7. Step 7: Revise the instruction.

5. Evaluation vs. Assessment vs. Research

Assessment Evaluation Research
Focus Learners' learning Quality/worth of instruction New generalisable knowledge
Purpose Grades and feedback to learners Improve and decide about instruction Test theories; publish findings
Example Final exam Review of the course design after delivery Study of whether gamification improves retention

Topic 14: Emerging Trends and Research in Instructional Design

Week 14 | AI in education, adaptive learning, gamification, micro-credentials, future directions

Overview

Instructional design is changing quickly because of new technologies and new learner needs. A modern designer must understand these trends, weigh their benefits and risks, and apply them wisely. This topic also prepares you to read and use research in the field.

Remember the key message of the course: technology is a tool; good pedagogy and evidence guide its use.

1. Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Education

Definition: Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems able to perform tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising patterns and making recommendations. Generative AI (for example, large language models) creates new text, images, audio and code.

Uses in instructional design

  • Content generation: drafting objectives, quiz questions, summaries, case studies and examples (requires expert review).
  • Intelligent tutoring systems: give hints and feedback tailored to the learner.
  • Chatbots/virtual assistants: answer learners' questions 24/7.
  • Automated feedback and grading for some tasks.
  • Learning analytics and prediction: identify learners at risk.
  • Translation, transcription and captioning, improving accessibility.
  • Personalised learning paths.

Concerns and ethics

  • Accuracy: AI may produce errors or invented ("hallucinated") information. Always verify.
  • Academic integrity: learners may misuse AI; redesign assessments to focus on process, reflection and authentic tasks.
  • Bias and fairness: AI may reflect biases in its training data.
  • Privacy and data protection.
  • Over-dependence: may weaken critical thinking if misused.
  • Digital divide: unequal access to AI tools.
  • Transparency and responsible use: declare where AI was used.
Example: A designer uses AI to draft 20 quiz questions for a Moodle module, then edits them, checks accuracy with a subject expert, and ensures they match the objectives. AI helped speed up the work; human judgement kept quality.
Key Point: AI should support, not replace, the instructional designer and the teacher. The designer remains responsible for quality, ethics and alignment.

2. Adaptive and Personalised Learning

Definition: Adaptive learning systems use data about each learner's performance to adjust content, pace and difficulty automatically.
  • Learners who master a topic move on; those who struggle get extra practice or alternative explanations.
  • Based on analytics and rules or AI algorithms.
  • Personalised learning: tailoring learning to the learner's needs, interests and pace (a broader idea).
  • Differentiated instruction: teacher adjusts content, process or product for different learners.
Example: A maths app gives easier questions after a learner makes mistakes, and harder ones after correct answers.

Benefits: efficient learning, targeted support, immediate feedback.
Challenges: cost, data privacy, quality of algorithms, need for reliable technology, risk of learners being "boxed" by the system.

3. Gamification and Game-Based Learning

Gamification Game-based learning
Meaning Adding game elements to non-game learning contexts Using a real game to teach content
Elements Points, badges, levels, leaderboards, progress bars, challenges, rewards Full games or simulations with goals and rules
Example Earning badges for completing LMS modules A simulation game where learners manage a virtual school network
  • Benefits: motivation, engagement, immediate feedback, persistence.
  • Risks: may encourage competition over learning; extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation; poor design can be trivial.
  • Good practice: link game elements to learning objectives, and use meaningful goals.

4. Micro-credentials and Digital Badges

Definition: A micro-credential is a short, focused, verified certification of specific skills or competencies, often earned through a small learning unit. A digital badge is an online representation of an achievement with verifiable information.
  • Flexible, stackable (can combine into larger qualifications), skill-focused.
  • Useful for lifelong learning and employers.
  • Quality, recognition and standards remain challenges.

6. Research in Instructional Design

Designers should base decisions on evidence, and sometimes carry out research themselves.

  • Evidence-based practice: use research findings to guide design.
  • Design-based research (DBR): research done through repeated design cycles in real settings; improves both the product and theory.
  • Action research: a practitioner studies and improves their own practice.
  • Common methods: experimental/quasi-experimental studies, surveys, case studies, mixed methods.
  • Key journals: Educational Technology Research and Development, British Journal of Educational Technology, Contemporary Educational Technology, Journal of Computing in Higher Education.

Technology acceptance and models used in research

  • TAM (Technology Acceptance Model): perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use influence whether people accept technology.
  • TPACK: framework for teachers' knowledge in technology integration.
  • SAMR (Puentedura): Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition; levels of technology integration.

7. Future Directions

  • More human-AI collaboration in design and teaching.
  • Greater personalisation and data-informed design.
  • Emphasis on skills for the future: critical thinking, creativity, digital literacy, collaboration.
  • Focus on inclusion, accessibility and equity.
  • More short, flexible, lifelong learning pathways.
  • Stronger attention to ethics, privacy and responsible AI.
  • Need for designers to stay current and to reflect on practice.

This topic also gives you a practical guide to preparing the project, the presentation, the reflective essay and the final exam.

2. The Instructional Design Project

Part 1: Design document (25%)

  • Title and context: the instructional problem and setting.
  • Needs analysis: gap, causes and evidence; the goal.
  • Learner analysis and context analysis.
  • Learning objectives (ABCD/Mager, Bloom verbs).
  • Assessment plan: formative and summative items, rubric.
  • Instructional strategies (Gagné/Merrill) and media/technology selection with reasons.
  • Content outline and sequence.
  • Evaluation plan (formative and summative; Kirkpatrick).
  • References (APA 7th edition).

Part 2: Prototype and presentation (35%)

  • A working prototype: an LMS module, e-learning storyboard or interactive learning object.
  • Evidence of multimedia principles and UDL/accessibility.
  • A short evaluation plan and results from a pilot test if possible.
  • A clear, time-limited presentation.
Key Point: Alignment is what examiners look for: objectives, assessment and activities must match. State this clearly in the document and the presentation.

3. Giving a Good Presentation

  • Structure: problem -> analysis -> design decisions -> prototype demonstration -> evaluation plan -> lessons learned.
  • Show the product; do not only talk about it.
  • Keep slides simple (apply coherence and signalling principles).
  • Justify decisions with theories and models from the course.
  • Practise and keep to the time limit.
  • Prepare for questions: "Why did you choose this model?", "How does this align with your objectives?", "How is it accessible?"

4. Peer Feedback

Good feedback is specific, kind, useful and linked to criteria.

Weak feedback Strong feedback
"Nice job." "Your objectives use clear verbs and match your quiz, which shows good alignment."
"The slides are bad." "The slide has too much text; try one image and three words to apply the coherence principle."
"I do not like it." "The navigation was unclear in Module 2; consider adding a Next button and a progress bar."
  • A useful model is "Two Stars and a Wish": two strengths and one suggestion.
  • Receivers should listen, ask clarifying questions and decide what to change.

5. Reflective Practice (Final Reflective Essay, 10%)

Reflection means thinking critically about your experiences in order to learn from them. A useful framework is:

  • What? What did I do and learn? (the project, key models, tools)
  • So what? Why does it matter? What changed in my thinking and skills?
  • Now what? How will I apply this in my school, college or workplace? What will I improve?
  • Use first person and be honest.
  • Link your reflection to theories and course ideas.
  • Include specific examples and challenges.
  • Discuss the future of ID in your own professional context (for example, in Ghana).

Exam tips

  • Read the question carefully and identify the command word: define, explain, compare, discuss, justify, apply, evaluate.
  • "Compare" needs similarities and differences; a table helps.
  • Support answers with examples from education, ICT and your own context.
  • Name theorists and models correctly (for example, Mayer, Gagné, Merrill, Kirkpatrick, Garrison).
  • Plan time and structure answers with introduction, body and conclusion.
  • Revise using the "What I Must Remember" sections and practise the MCQs.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• The course follows ADDIE: Analysis (Topics 2-3), Design (4-9), Development (10-11), Implementation (12), Evaluation (13), Future trends (14).
• The project: Part 1 design document (25%) and Part 2 prototype and presentation (35%); other components: discussions 10%, analysis paper 20%, reflection 10%.
• Presentation: show the product, justify decisions with theory, stay within time, and prepare for questions.
• Peer feedback should be specific, kind and criteria-based ("Two Stars and a Wish").
• Reflection: What? So what? Now what?
• Exam: know the command words, compare with tables, support with examples, name theorists correctly, and revise the summaries and MCQs.
