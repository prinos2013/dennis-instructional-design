Course Summary Note

What Is This Course About?

Instructional Design and Development using Technology (MIT 601) teaches you how to plan, build, deliver and improve teaching and learning experiences in a systematic way, using digital tools.

Instead of teaching "by instinct", you learn to follow a process: find out what learners need, state clear objectives, design suitable assessments and activities, choose the right media, build the materials, deliver them, and evaluate the results.

The course sits at the heart of the M.Ed. Information Technology programme because it joins three things: pedagogy (how people learn), content (what is taught) and technology (the tools used).

Graduates are expected to lead e-learning and digital transformation in schools, colleges, training institutions and workplaces.

Why Is It Important?

  • It makes teaching effective, efficient and engaging.
  • It helps you design online, blended and technology-enhanced courses (for example, on Moodle).
  • It trains you to make decisions using theory and evidence, not guesswork.
  • It prepares you for careers as instructional designers, e-learning developers, ICT coordinators and educational technology leaders.

Topic 1: Introduction to Instructional Design

Week 1 | Definitions, the designer's role, systematic vs. systemic approaches, contexts

Overview

Imagine a teacher who wants students to understand how a computer network works. One way is to walk into class and simply talk. Another way is to first find out what students already know, decide exactly what they should be able to do at the end, choose activities and tools that help them reach that goal, and then check whether it worked. The second way is instructional design (ID).

Instructional design is the planned, step-by-step way of creating teaching and learning experiences so that learners actually learn what they are supposed to learn. It is the foundation of the whole MED-MIT course: every later topic (theories, models, objectives, assessment, media, online courses, evaluation) is a part of this big idea.

1. Basic Definitions

Instruction is the deliberate arrangement of information, activities and resources to help someone learn.

Instructional design is the systematic process of analysing learning needs, designing and developing instruction to meet those needs, and evaluating whether it worked.

Instructional technology is the use of tools (computers, software, mobile phones, projectors, learning platforms) to support teaching and learning.

Do not confuse teaching with instructional design. Teaching is what happens when the teacher and learners interact. Instructional design is the planning and preparation behind it. A good lesson is usually the result of good design.

Example: A lecturer who prepares a Moodle module on "Introduction to Databases" (with objectives, readings, a video, a quiz and a discussion forum) is doing instructional design. Delivering the lecture live is teaching.

2. Why Instructional Design Matters

  • Effectiveness: learners actually achieve the intended outcomes.
  • Efficiency: time, money and effort are not wasted on unnecessary content.
  • Appeal (engagement): learners stay motivated.
  • Consistency: many teachers can deliver the same quality of instruction.
  • Accountability: results can be measured and improved.
Key Point: Good instruction is often described by three words: effective, efficient and engaging.

3. The Role of the Instructional Designer

An instructional designer is the person who applies ID principles to build learning experiences. In practice the role is wide:

Role What the designer does
AnalystFinds the problem, studies learners and context
Learning architectWrites objectives and plans the structure of the course
Content developerCreates or organises notes, videos, quizzes, activities
Technology integratorChooses tools such as an LMS, H5P or Articulate
EvaluatorCollects feedback and data and improves the design
Project manager / collaboratorWorks with subject-matter experts (SMEs), teachers, graphic designers and IT staff
Example: A university wants to move a face-to-face course online. The designer meets the subject expert, studies the students' internet access, rewrites the objectives for online study, builds the Moodle pages, and plans how to test the course.

4. Systematic vs. Systemic Approaches

These two words look alike but mean different things, and they are a common exam question.

Feature Systematic Systemic
Meaning Following an orderly, step-by-step procedure Looking at the whole system and how its parts affect one another
Focus The process (do step 1, then 2, then 3) The environment and connections (school, learners, policy, resources)
Question asked "Have we followed the steps in order?" "How will this design work within the whole organisation?"
Example Following ADDIE phase by phase Considering how a new e-learning course affects timetable, staff workload and internet cost
Key Point: ID is both systematic (ordered steps) and systemic (every part is linked, and a change in one part affects the others).

6. Brief Foundations of the Field

  • During the Second World War, the military needed to train many people quickly. Psychologists and educators designed systematic training, which began modern ID.
  • Robert Gagné, Robert Mager, Benjamin Bloom and others shaped objectives, learning conditions and taxonomies.
  • ADDIE and similar models grew from this and became the standard in education and training.
  • Today, computers, the internet, mobile learning and AI have expanded the field into e-learning and digital transformation.

7. Common Misunderstandings

  • ID is not just making PowerPoint slides.
  • ID is not only for online courses; it applies to any teaching.
  • Technology is not the goal. Learning is the goal; technology is a means.
  • A beautiful course that does not help learners achieve the objectives is poorly designed.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• Instructional design = the systematic process of analysing needs, designing, developing, implementing and evaluating instruction.
• Good instruction is effective, efficient and engaging.
• Systematic = step-by-step procedure; systemic = the whole system and its connected parts.
• Teaching is delivery; ID is the planning behind it. Technology is a tool, not the goal.
• Designer roles: analyst, learning architect, content developer, technology integrator, evaluator, collaborator.
• ID is applied in K-12, higher education, corporate and health professions, and non-formal settings.
• ID grew from wartime training needs and the work of Gagné, Mager, Bloom and others.

Topic 2: Foundations: Learning Theories and Instructional Design

Week 2 | Behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism, connectivism, memory and learning

Overview

A learning theory is an explanation of how people learn. Instructional designers need these theories because the way you believe learning happens decides how you design instruction. If you believe learning is remembering facts, you will design drills. If you believe learning is building understanding with others, you will design projects and discussions.

Four major theories are examined in this course: behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism and connectivism. Each one adds a useful lens. A good designer does not fight over which is "right" but selects the one that suits the type of learning needed.

1. Behaviourism

Definition: Behaviourism views learning as a change in observable behaviour caused by stimuli in the environment and the consequences that follow.
  • Key thinkers: Pavlov (classical conditioning), Thorndike (law of effect), Skinner (operant conditioning).
  • The learner is seen as passive: the environment shapes the learner.
  • Main ideas: stimulus-response, reinforcement (increases behaviour), punishment (reduces behaviour), practice and repetition, immediate feedback.

Implications for design: Break content into small steps and give clear, measurable objectives. Give immediate feedback and rewards (marks, badges, praise). Use drill and practice for basic skills such as multiplication tables, typing and spelling.

Example: A typing-tutor app shows a letter, the learner types it, and the app instantly says "Correct!" and gives points. This is behaviourist design.

2. Cognitivism

Definition: Cognitivism views learning as an internal mental process: receiving, organising, storing and retrieving information in the mind.
  • Key thinkers: Piaget (cognitive development), Bruner, Ausubel (meaningful learning and advance organisers), Gagné (information processing), Sweller (cognitive load).
  • The learner is an active processor of information. The mind is compared to a computer: input, processing, storage and output.
  • Main ideas: attention, memory, prior knowledge, organisation of information, cognitive load.

Implications for design: Gain attention, link new content to what learners already know. Organise content logically (simple to complex) and use diagrams, outlines and concept maps. Avoid overloading learners with too much information at once. Provide practice that helps move information into long-term memory.

Example: Before teaching "relational databases", a lecturer first reviews spreadsheets (which learners know), then shows a diagram of linked tables. This builds on prior knowledge.

3. Constructivism

Definition: Constructivism views learning as an active process in which learners build their own knowledge and meaning from experience and interaction.
  • Key thinkers: Piaget (individual construction), Vygotsky (social construction), Dewey and Bruner.
  • Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with help. The help given is called scaffolding.
  • The teacher is a facilitator or guide; the learner is active and responsible.
  • Learning is best when tasks are real, meaningful and collaborative.

Implications for design: Use problem-based learning, projects, case studies and group work. Use authentic tasks that look like real life. Encourage reflection, discussion and peer learning.

Example: Student teachers work in groups to design a lesson for a real school, discuss on an online forum, and revise their lesson after feedback. They are constructing knowledge together.

4. Connectivism

Definition: Connectivism (Siemens, Downes) views learning as the ability to build and move through networks of people, information and technology in the digital age.
  • Knowledge lives in networks (websites, databases, communities, social media), not only in one person's head.
  • "Knowing where to find information" can be as important as knowing the information itself.
  • Learning includes forming and maintaining connections; information changes quickly so currency (being up to date) is vital.

Implications for design: Use blogs, wikis, social networks, MOOCs, open educational resources and professional learning networks. Teach digital literacy: searching, filtering and judging information. Encourage learners to curate and share resources.

Example: A teacher joins a WhatsApp/LinkedIn community of ICT teachers across Africa, follows experts, shares resources and solves classroom problems with the group. That is connectivist learning.

5. Comparing the Four Theories

Behaviourism Cognitivism Constructivism Connectivism
Learning is Change in behaviour Mental processing Building meaning Forming network connections
Learner is Passive responder Active processor Active constructor Active network participant
Teacher is Controller/trainer Organiser of information Facilitator Guide/network co-learner
Typical activity Drill, quiz, reward Diagrams, organisers, chunking Projects, case studies Blogs, MOOCs, communities
Best for Basic facts and skills Concepts, memory, problem-solving Complex, real-world problems Fast-changing digital knowledge

6. Memory and Learning

The information-processing model explains how information moves through memory:

Stage Description Design tip
Sensory memory Very brief (seconds) store for what we see and hear Gain attention; make important points stand out
Working (short-term) memory Holds a small amount of information (about 4-7 items) for a short time while we think Chunk information; avoid overload
Long-term memory Large, lasting store organised in schemas (mental structures) Link to prior knowledge; use practice, review and spacing
  • Cognitive load is the mental effort used in working memory. Good design reduces unnecessary load.
  • Schema: an organised mental framework (for example, your idea of what a "classroom" is).
  • Transfer: applying what is learned in a new situation, the real aim of instruction.
Key Point: No single theory is best. A skilled designer matches the theory to the learning goal: behaviourism for basic skills, cognitivism for understanding concepts, constructivism for complex tasks, connectivism for networked, changing knowledge.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• Behaviourism: observable behaviour, stimulus-response, reinforcement, drill and feedback (Pavlov, Thorndike, Skinner).
• Cognitivism: mental processing, memory, prior knowledge, cognitive load (Piaget, Bruner, Ausubel, Gagné, Sweller).
• Constructivism: learners build knowledge; ZPD and scaffolding (Vygotsky); projects and collaboration.
• Connectivism: learning in networks; digital age (Siemens, Downes).
• Memory stages: sensory, working (limited), long-term (schemas). Reduce cognitive load.
• Match the theory to the learning goal; no single theory is best.
