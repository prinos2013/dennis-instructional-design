Topic 3: Needs Analysis and Learner Analysis

Week 3 | Needs assessment, instructional goals, learner characteristics, context analysis

Overview

Before building anything, a designer must ask: "Is there really a problem, and is training or instruction the solution?" A doctor does not prescribe medicine before diagnosis. In the same way, an instructional designer does not start creating a course before doing analysis. This is the Analysis phase of ADDIE and the foundation of the design.

Three analyses are covered: needs analysis (what is the gap?), learner analysis (who are the learners?) and context analysis (where and how will learning happen?).

1. Needs Analysis (Needs Assessment)

Definition: A need is a gap between the current state ("what is") and the desired state ("what should be"). Needs assessment is the process of identifying and measuring that gap.
Example: Teachers in a school should be able to upload lesson notes to the school LMS (desired state), but only 20% can do it (current state). The 80% gap is the need.

Not every problem needs instruction: Gaps may be caused by lack of knowledge/skills (instruction can help) or by other causes such as poor motivation, no tools, unclear policies or poor management (instruction alone will not help). The designer must find the real cause (performance analysis or root-cause analysis) before deciding on a solution.

Types of needs (Kaufman / Burton and Merrill)

  • Normative need: compared with a standard (for example, national ICT standard).
  • Felt need: what people say they want.
  • Expressed need: needs shown through action (requests, complaints, enrolments).
  • Comparative need: one group compared with another.
  • Anticipated (future) need: needs expected because of coming changes such as a new curriculum.

Methods of collecting data

Method Use
Questionnaires / surveysReach many people quickly
InterviewsGet deep opinions from key people
Focus groupsGroup discussion of problems and ideas
ObservationSee actual performance
Document and data reviewResults, reports, policies, past records
Tests/pre-assessmentMeasure existing knowledge and skills

Steps in a simple needs assessment

  • Step 1: Define the desired performance or standard.
  • Step 2: Find the current performance.
  • Step 3: Identify the gap.
  • Step 4: Find causes of the gap.
  • Step 5: Rank needs by importance and urgency.
  • Step 6: Decide whether instruction is the right solution and set priorities.

2. Identifying Instructional Goals

Definition: An instructional goal is a broad statement of what learners will be able to do after instruction.
  • It comes from the needs analysis.
  • It is written in terms of what learners will do, not what the teacher will do.
  • It is later broken down into specific, measurable objectives (see Topic 6).
Example: Goal: "Teachers will be able to use Moodle to deliver a course online." (Not: "To teach teachers about Moodle.")

3. Learner Analysis

Learner analysis asks, "Who will be learning?" Instruction that suits one group may fail with another.

Learner characteristic Questions to ask Design effect
Prior knowledge and skills (entry behaviours) What do they already know? Do they have prerequisite skills? Decide starting point; add review or pre-training
Age and education level Are they adults, teenagers, graduates? Choose language, examples and approach (adults prefer practical, self-directed learning)
Motivation and attitude Why are they learning? Willing or forced? Add relevance, goals and incentives
Learning preferences Do they prefer video, text, discussion, hands-on? Offer multiple formats
Digital skills and access Do they have devices, data, electricity, computer skills? Choose low-bandwidth or offline options where needed
Language and culture What language is used? Cultural background? Use clear language and culturally relevant examples
Special needs Visual, hearing, mobility or learning challenges? Apply accessibility and UDL principles
Key Point: About "learning styles" (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic): research does not strongly support matching teaching to one style. It is better to use multiple representations (words, pictures, practice) for everyone.

Adult learners (Knowles' andragogy)

  • Adults are self-directed and want to know why they are learning.
  • They bring rich experience.
  • They want problem-centred, practical learning.
  • They are motivated by internal reasons such as promotion and growth.

4. Context Analysis

Context analysis studies the environment in which learning will happen (orienting context) and where learning will be applied (performance context).

  • Learning context: classroom, computer lab, home, workplace, online; time available; infrastructure; internet reliability; electricity; technical support.
  • Performance (transfer) context: where learners will use the skills, for example in their own schools; whether supervisors support the use.
  • Organisational context: policies, budget, management support, culture of change.
Example: A teacher training in a rural area: poor internet and few laptops. The designer prepares printable notes, offline-downloadable videos and WhatsApp support instead of live video classes.

5. Putting the Analysis Together

The results are combined in an analysis report that states the problem, the gap, causes, learner profile, context description, constraints and the recommended goal. This report guides objectives, assessments, strategies and technology choices.

Analysis Main question Output
Needs analysisWhat is the gap, and is instruction the answer?Prioritised needs and goals
Learner analysisWho are the learners?Learner profile
Context analysisWhere and under what conditions?Constraints and resources list
Task / content analysisWhat must be learned (skills and knowledge)?Breakdown of content and tasks
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• Need = gap between "what is" and "what should be".
• Find the cause of the gap; instruction solves only knowledge/skill gaps.
• Types of need: normative, felt, expressed, comparative, anticipated.
• Data methods: surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, document review, tests.
• Instructional goal = broad statement of what learners will be able to do.
• Learner analysis: prior knowledge, age, motivation, preferences, digital access, language, special needs.
• Context analysis: learning, performance and organisational contexts.
• Analysis results feed all later design decisions.

Topic 4: Instructional Design Models I: The ADDIE Model

Week 4 | Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation; strengths and limitations

Overview

An instructional design model is a framework or map that guides the designer through the process of creating instruction. Just as an architect follows a building plan, a designer follows a model so that nothing important is forgotten.

ADDIE is the most widely known generic ID model. The name is formed from its five phases: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation and Evaluation. Many other models are variations of it. The exact origin of ADDIE is unclear (Molenda, 2003 describes it as "elusive"), but it is the standard reference point in the field.

1. The Five Phases of ADDIE

Phase Key question Main activities Output (deliverable)
Analysis What needs to be learned, by whom, and where? Needs, learner, context, task and content analysis; set goals Analysis report; learner profile; goals
Design How will we teach it? Write objectives; design assessments; choose strategies and media; sequence content; storyboards Design document; blueprint; assessment plan
Development Let's build it. Produce materials, videos, slides, quizzes, LMS pages; test and revise (alpha/beta) Complete instructional materials; prototype
Implementation Deliver it. Train teachers/facilitators; set up LMS; deliver instruction; provide support Delivered course; learners in action
Evaluation Did it work? Formative evaluation (during) and summative evaluation (after); gather data; revise Evaluation report; improvement list
Key Point: Evaluation is not only the last step. In practice, formative evaluation takes place in every phase, so the process is iterative: you may return to an earlier phase when problems are found.

2. Each Phase in Simple Detail

A: Analysis
Find out the problem and the gap. Study learners and context. Decide whether instruction is the answer and state the goal.

D: Design

  • Write clear, measurable learning objectives.
  • Plan how you will assess whether objectives were achieved.
  • Select strategies (for example, case studies, demonstration, group work).
  • Select media and technology and decide on the structure and sequence.
  • Prepare a design document and storyboards.

D: Development

  • Create the actual content: slides, videos, e-learning modules, worksheets, quizzes.
  • Build in the LMS or authoring tool.
  • Do pilot testing, then revise.

I: Implementation

  • Deliver the course to real learners.
  • Train instructors and support staff; prepare learners (orientation).
  • Manage logistics: venues, devices, internet, schedules, helpdesk.

E: Evaluation

  • Formative: to improve while the design is still being developed or delivered (pilot tests, expert reviews, learner feedback).
  • Summative: to judge the final effectiveness (results, satisfaction, impact on performance).
Example: ADDIE in a school ICT project. Analysis: Form 2 students cannot create spreadsheets. Design: objectives, a hands-on activity plan, a quiz. Development: build tutorial videos and worksheets. Implementation: teach two classes in the ICT lab. Evaluation: student scores and feedback; revise the video length.

3. Strengths of ADDIE

  • Simple, logical and easy to remember.
  • Generic: it works for schools, training, e-learning and many other settings.
  • Ensures learners and needs are analysed before building.
  • Encourages evaluation and continuous improvement.
  • Provides clear deliverables at each stage, helpful for project management.
  • A base for other models.

4. Limitations (Criticisms) of ADDIE

  • When treated as strictly linear ("waterfall"), it is slow and inflexible.
  • Changes late in the project are costly if a problem is discovered after development.
  • It can lead to heavy documentation.
  • Stakeholders and learners may see the product only at the end.
  • It is a general framework, not detailed about how to design each step (it tells what, not exactly how).
  • Critics (including Molenda) say there is no single official version of ADDIE.
Key Point: These weaknesses led to rapid prototyping and agile approaches such as SAM (see Topic 5). The modern view is to use ADDIE iteratively, not as a rigid waterfall.

5. Linear vs. Iterative ADDIE

Waterfall (linear) ADDIE Iterative ADDIE
Flow Finish one phase before starting the next Move between phases; repeat cycles
Feedback Mostly at the end Throughout the process
Risk Errors found late Errors found early
Suits Stable, well-defined projects Complex or changing projects
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• ADDIE: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation.
• Deliverables: analysis report -> design document -> prototype/materials -> delivered course -> evaluation report.
• Formative evaluation = to improve during the process; summative = to judge the final result.
• Strengths: simple, generic, systematic, encourages evaluation.
• Limitations: can be slow, rigid if linear, late discovery of errors, no single official version (Molenda, 2003).
• Modern practice: use ADDIE iteratively. Rapid prototyping and SAM respond to its limits.

Topic 5: Instructional Design Models II: Dick and Carey, Kemp, SAM and Rapid Prototyping

Week 5 | Systems approach, Kemp model, Successive Approximation Model

Overview

Different projects need different models. Some models are very detailed and step-by-step (Dick and Carey); some are flexible and learner-centred (Kemp); others are fast and repeated (SAM, rapid prototyping). A professional designer should be able to compare them and choose the one that fits the context.

1. The Dick and Carey Systems Approach Model

Developed by Walter Dick and Lou Carey, this model treats instruction as a system in which learner, instructor, materials and environment all interact. It focuses strongly on the link between objectives, instruction and assessment. It has ten components:

No. Component Meaning
1Identify instructional goalsWhat should learners be able to do?
2Conduct instructional analysisBreak the goal into steps and skills
3Analyse learners and contextsEntry skills, characteristics, setting
4Write performance objectivesSpecific, measurable outcomes
5Develop assessment instrumentsTests/rubrics matched to objectives
6Develop instructional strategyActivities, grouping, delivery and sequence
7Develop and select instructional materialsCreate or choose suitable materials
8Design and conduct formative evaluationTry out and improve while developing
9Revise instructionImprove based on feedback and data
10Design and conduct summative evaluationJudge the overall value of the instruction
  • Strengths: very thorough; strong alignment of objectives, strategies and tests; good for content-heavy training.
  • Limitations: looks linear and heavy; can be time-consuming; may feel too rigid for fast projects.
Key Point: Notice that the Dick and Carey model puts the assessment (step 5) before the strategy (step 6): you decide how you will measure success before you decide how to teach. This is called "backward" thinking.

2. The Kemp Model (Morrison, Ross and Kemp)

Kemp's model is flexible and non-linear. It is drawn as an oval with elements that can be done in any order and revisited. It focuses strongly on the learner and the whole programme (planning, revision and evaluation are continuous).

  • Nine elements: identify instructional problems and goals; examine learner characteristics; identify subject content and analyse task components; state instructional objectives; sequence content; design instructional strategies; plan the instructional message and delivery; develop evaluation instruments; select resources to support learning.
  • Surrounding processes: planning, project management, support services, formative evaluation, summative evaluation, confirmative evaluation, revision.
  • Confirmative evaluation checks whether the instruction continues to be effective over time.
  • Strengths: flexible; learner-centred; allows any starting point; continuous revision.
  • Limitations: less structured for beginners; may need a skilled designer to manage the flexibility.

3. Rapid Prototyping

Definition: Rapid prototyping is the approach of quickly building a simple working version (prototype) of the instruction, testing it with users, and improving it repeatedly.
  • Learners and clients see something early, so problems are found early.
  • It saves time and cost compared with building everything before testing.
  • Useful for e-learning, apps and complex or uncertain projects.
Example: Instead of building a 10-module Moodle course, the team builds Module 1 quickly, tests it with five students, improves it, and then continues.

4. SAM: Successive Approximation Model (Michael Allen)

SAM is an agile model that grew from criticism of ADDIE. Instead of perfect planning followed by one big build, SAM works in short, repeated cycles with continuous collaboration and review. "Successive approximation" means getting closer and closer to the final product by repeated improvements.

SAM1 (simpler, for small projects)

  • Evaluate (gather information, background).
  • Design (plan and prototype).
  • Develop (build and review).
  • Repeat the design-develop cycle until the product is ready.

SAM2 (for larger projects) has three phases

Phase What happens
Preparation Gather information and hold a savvy start, a collaborative brainstorming session with the team and stakeholders to share ideas and create early prototypes
Iterative Design Project planning, then repeated cycles of design, prototype and review
Iterative Development Repeated cycles of develop, implement and evaluate until the final release (the "gold" version)
  • Strengths: fast, collaborative, flexible, lowers risk, stakeholders involved early.
  • Limitations: needs frequent stakeholder availability; can lead to "scope creep" (the project keeps growing); needs good project management.

5. Comparing the Models

Feature ADDIE Dick & Carey Kemp SAM
Nature Generic, 5 phases Systems approach, 10 steps Flexible, non-linear Agile, iterative
Structure Mostly sequential (can iterate) Sequential with feedback loops Any order, continuous revision Short repeated cycles
Speed Moderate to slow Slower Moderate Fast
Best for General projects, beginners Detailed training with strong alignment Learner-focused programmes Rapid e-learning, uncertain requirements
Main criticism Can be rigid Heavy and time-consuming Needs expertise Needs constant collaboration

How to choose a model

  • Consider time and budget: tight deadlines favour SAM / rapid prototyping.
  • Consider clarity of requirements: clear requirements suit ADDIE or Dick & Carey; unclear ones suit SAM.
  • Consider team experience: beginners benefit from structured models.
  • Consider project size and risk: high risk benefits from early prototyping.
  • Often designers combine models.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• Dick & Carey: 10 components; systems view; strong alignment of objectives-assessment-strategy; formative then summative evaluation.
• Kemp: flexible, non-linear, learner-centred, continuous planning and revision; includes confirmative evaluation.
• Rapid prototyping: build quickly, test early, improve repeatedly.
• SAM (Allen): agile; successive approximation; SAM1 (Evaluate, Design, Develop) and SAM2 (Preparation, Iterative Design, Iterative Development); savvy start.
• Choose a model according to time, budget, clarity of requirements, team skills and risk.
• Be ready to compare models in a table and justify your choice.
