Topic 9: Multimedia Learning and Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

Week 9 | Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning, cognitive load, UDL guidelines

Overview

Today, much learning content mixes words and pictures: slides, videos, animations and e-learning modules. But more media does not automatically mean better learning. Poorly designed multimedia can confuse and overload learners. This topic explains how people learn from words and pictures and how to design media so that all learners, including those with disabilities, can learn.

Two frameworks are central: Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) and Universal Design for Learning (UDL).

1. Multimedia Learning: Basic Idea

Definition: Multimedia learning is learning from words (spoken or written) and pictures (illustrations, photos, animations, video). Richard Mayer's simple statement: people learn better from words and pictures than from words alone.

2. Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)

CTML rests on three assumptions about how the mind works:

Assumption Meaning
Dual channelsWe process visual/pictorial material and auditory/verbal material in two separate channels
Limited capacityEach channel can process only a small amount at one time
Active processingLearning requires attending, organising information and integrating it with prior knowledge

The learner selects relevant words and images, organises them into coherent mental models, and integrates them with existing knowledge stored in long-term memory.

Three types of cognitive processing demands

Type Meaning Design aim
ExtraneousWasted effort caused by poor design (clutter, distractions)Reduce
EssentialEffort to understand the core material (its complexity)Manage (for example, by chunking)
GenerativeDeeper effort to make sense of the materialFoster (motivation, practice, questions)

3. Mayer's Principles of Multimedia Design

A. Principles for reducing extraneous processing

Principle Meaning Example
CoherenceRemove unnecessary words, music and imagesDelete decorative clip art and background music
SignallingHighlight the essential materialBold key terms, arrows, colour
RedundancyDo not add on-screen text that repeats narrationNarrate a diagram; do not also place the same full sentences
Spatial contiguityPlace related words and pictures near each otherLabels next to diagram parts, not in a legend far away
Temporal contiguityPresent related words and pictures at the same timeNarration plays while the animation shows the same step

B. Principles for managing essential processing

Principle Meaning Example
SegmentingBreak lessons into learner-paced partsShort video chunks with a "Next" button
Pre-trainingTeach key terms/concepts firstIntroduce component names before the animation
ModalityUse graphics with narration rather than on-screen textSpoken explanation of a diagram

C. Principles for fostering generative processing

Principle Meaning Example
MultimediaWords + pictures are better than words aloneAdd a diagram to a text explanation
PersonalisationUse conversational language"You will now create..." rather than formal passive text
VoiceA friendly human voice is better than a machine voiceTeacher records narration
EmbodimentHuman-like gestures in videosInstructor points to elements
ImageInstructor's image on screen is not always necessaryUse only if it helps
Key Point: Easy way to remember the big three groups: Reduce clutter, Manage complexity, Foster deep thinking.

4. Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller)

  • Working memory is limited, so too much information at once causes overload.
  • Intrinsic load: difficulty of the content itself.
  • Extraneous load: caused by poor design; reduce it.
  • Germane load: effort that builds schemas; encourage it.
Example: A slide with 120 words, three fonts, animation and background music overloads the learner. A redesigned slide with one diagram, three labelled points and spoken explanation lowers the load.

5. Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

Definition: UDL is a framework (developed by CAST) for designing learning experiences that are accessible and effective for all learners from the start, rather than adjusting for individuals later.

The idea comes from architecture: a ramp built into a building from the beginning helps wheelchair users, parents with prams and people carrying loads. In the same way, captions in videos help deaf learners, second-language learners and learners in noisy places.

Three principles of UDL

Principle The "why/what/how" Means Examples
Engagement The WHY of learning Multiple means of engagement: motivate and sustain interest Choice of topics, relevant tasks, collaboration, goal-setting
Representation The WHAT of learning Multiple means of representation: present information in different ways Text + audio + video + diagrams, captions, glossaries
Action and Expression The HOW of learning Multiple means of action and expression: let learners show what they know in different ways Write, record, build, present; flexible tools
  • The CAST guidelines (version 2.2, 2018) organise each principle into guidelines and checkpoints, such as options for perception, language, comprehension, physical action, expression, executive function, recruiting interest and self-regulation.
  • Accessibility (legal and technical) focuses on removing barriers; UDL is broader and focuses on flexibility and learner variability.

Practical accessibility checklist

  • Provide alt text for images.
  • Provide captions and transcripts for videos and audio.
  • Use sufficient colour contrast and do not use colour alone to give meaning.
  • Use proper headings and clear, readable fonts.
  • Make materials usable with keyboard and screen readers.
  • Provide content in multiple formats and keep language simple.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• CTML assumptions: dual channels, limited capacity, active processing.
• Three processing demands: extraneous (reduce), essential (manage), generative (foster).
• Reduce: coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial and temporal contiguity. Manage: segmenting, pre-training, modality. Foster: multimedia, personalisation, voice, embodiment.
• Cognitive load: intrinsic, extraneous, germane.
• UDL (CAST): Engagement (why), Representation (what), Action and Expression (how).
• Accessibility basics: alt text, captions, transcripts, colour contrast, headings, keyboard access.

Topic 10: Developing Instructional Materials

Week 10 | Storyboarding, rapid e-learning, authoring tools, accessible documents and videos

Overview

Development is where the plan becomes a real product. In ADDIE this is the Development phase. Here the designer turns objectives, strategies and the design document into actual learning materials: slides, handouts, videos, e-learning modules, quizzes and LMS pages.

Good development follows careful planning (storyboards), uses suitable authoring tools, applies accessibility and multimedia principles, and includes testing and revision.

1. From Design to Development

  • Design document: describes objectives, content outline, strategies, assessments and media.
  • Storyboard: a visual plan of each screen or scene.
  • Prototype: an early, working sample for review.
  • Final product: the completed materials after testing and revision.

2. Storyboarding

Definition: A storyboard is a visual and textual blueprint that shows what will appear on each screen or video scene, in order, before production begins.

Storyboards help the team (designer, subject expert, graphic artist, developer) agree on the content and save time, because it is easier to change a sketch than a finished module.

Typical storyboard contents (per screen)

Element Description
Screen number and titleIdentifies the screen
Visuals / layoutSketch or description of images, charts, animation
On-screen textExact text learners will read
Audio / narration scriptWhat will be spoken
InteractionsButtons, drag-and-drop, quizzes, branching
FeedbackMessages after learners respond
NavigationWhere the learner goes next
Notes / accessibilityAlt text, captions, developer notes
  • Types: sketch-based (hand drawings), template-based (PowerPoint or Word tables), tool-based (digital storyboard tools).
  • Flowchart/ site map shows the overall structure and navigation of the module.
Example: Screen 4: Title "What is a Network?" Visual: diagram of three computers connected to a router. Narration: "A network is a group of connected devices..." Interaction: Learner drags labels to the diagram. Feedback: "Correct! The router connects devices."

3. Rapid E-Learning Development

Definition: Rapid e-learning is a way of producing e-learning quickly using templates, authoring tools and existing content (such as PowerPoint), often with subject experts developing content themselves.
  • Advantages: fast, cheaper, easy to update.
  • Risks: poor instructional quality if designers skip analysis; boring "slide-turning" courses; inconsistent quality.
  • Good practice: keep objectives and interaction clear, even when producing quickly.

4. Authoring Tools

Authoring tools help non-programmers produce interactive e-learning content.

Tool Type Typical use
Articulate Storyline / RiseCommercial, desktop and webInteractive modules, scenarios, quizzes; Rise for responsive lessons
Adobe CaptivateCommercialSoftware simulations, responsive e-learning
H5PFree, open source (plugin for Moodle, WordPress)Interactive videos, quizzes, drag-and-drop, flashcards, timelines
iSpring SuiteCommercialPowerPoint-based e-learning
Canva, GeniallyWeb toolsVisual content, infographics and interactive presentations
Camtasia, OBS StudioScreen recording and editingVideo tutorials (OBS is free)

Choosing an authoring tool

  • Learning needs (simple content or complex simulations?).
  • Cost and licence; free options such as H5P suit limited budgets.
  • Compatibility with the LMS: SCORM or xAPI (standards that let content run on and report to an LMS).
  • Learning curve and technical support.
  • Mobile-friendly (responsive) output.
  • Accessibility support.
Key Point: SCORM (Sharable Content Object Reference Model) is a standard packaging format that allows e-learning content to be uploaded to any compatible LMS and to report scores and completion.

5. Creating Accessible Documents and Videos

Material Accessibility actions
Word/PDF documentsUse built-in heading styles; add alt text to images; use real lists and simple tables with header rows; meaningful link text ("Download the guide", not "click here"); sufficient contrast
SlidesLarge readable fonts; limited words per slide; alt text; logical reading order
VideosAccurate captions (subtitles); transcript; audio description for important visual-only content; clear audio; avoid flashing content
Audio/podcastsProvide transcripts
Interactive modulesKeyboard navigation; labels; do not use colour alone to give meaning

6. Development Process: Quality and Testing

  • Style guide: fonts, colours, tone, naming rules to keep materials consistent.
  • Review by subject-matter expert (SME): for accuracy.
  • Editing and proofreading for language and clarity.
  • Alpha test: internal testing by the project team for technical errors.
  • Beta test (pilot): testing with a sample of real learners; collect feedback.
  • Revise and then finalise.

Copyright reminders during development

  • Use open educational resources (OER) and Creative Commons images where possible.
  • Give credit and respect licences.
  • Do not copy materials from the internet without permission.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• Storyboard: screen-by-screen plan with visuals, text, narration, interactions, feedback and navigation.
• Rapid e-learning: quick production with templates and authoring tools; watch quality.
• Tools: Articulate Storyline/Rise, Captivate, H5P (free), iSpring, Canva, Camtasia/OBS.
• SCORM/xAPI: standards for LMS compatibility and tracking.
• Accessibility: headings, alt text, captions, transcripts, contrast, meaningful links.
• Quality process: style guide, SME review, alpha test, beta/pilot test, revision.
• Respect copyright; prefer OER and Creative Commons.

Topic 11: Online Course Design

Week 11 | Designing for the LMS, structuring modules, engaging online activities, Community of Inquiry

Overview

An online course is not simply a face-to-face course uploaded to the internet. Online learners cannot see the teacher's face, cannot ask questions the instant they arise, and often study alone. A well-designed online course therefore provides clear structure, clear communication, interaction and support.

This topic covers the Learning Management System (LMS), how to organise an online course, how to create engaging activities, and the Community of Inquiry (Col) framework, which explains what makes online learning successful.

1. Learning Management Systems (LMS)

Definition: A Learning Management System (LMS) is a software platform for delivering, managing and tracking online learning. It combines content, communication, assessment and records in one place.
LMS Notes
MoodleFree, open-source; widely used by universities (including UCC's e-learning platform); highly customisable
CanvasModern, user-friendly; used by many universities; free and paid versions
Google ClassroomSimple and free with Google accounts; good for schools; fewer advanced features
Blackboard, Microsoft Teams for Education, Open edXOther platforms for institutions and MOOCs

Common LMS features

  • Course pages and content (files, pages, videos, links).
  • Communication: announcements, forums, messaging.
  • Assessment: quizzes, assignments, gradebook, rubrics.
  • Tracking: completion and activity reports.
  • User management and enrolment.
  • Integration with other tools (video conferencing, plagiarism checkers).

2. Structuring an Online Course

Principles of good structure

  • Consistency: the same layout for every module so learners know where to find things.
  • Chunking: divide content into small manageable units (weeks or modules).
  • Clarity: clear navigation and instructions.
  • Alignment: objectives, activities and assessments match.
  • Workload: realistic study time estimates.

A typical module template

Section Content
Module overviewIntroduction and why it matters
Learning objectivesWhat learners will be able to do
Learning materialsNotes, readings, videos, resources
Learning activitiesDiscussions, tasks, exercises, group work
AssessmentQuiz or assignment with due dates and rubric
Summary and next stepsWrap-up and preview of next module

Start-here (orientation) section

  • Welcome message and instructor introduction.
  • Course syllabus, schedule and assessment details.
  • Netiquette (rules of online communication).
  • How to get technical help and who to contact.
  • A short "get started" activity (introduce yourself).

3. Engaging Online Activities

Activity Purpose Tip
Discussion forumsReflection, peer learningUse thought-provoking questions; set clear expectations for posting and replying
Collaborative projectsTeamwork and applicationAssign roles; give clear instructions and timelines
Quizzes with feedbackPractice, self-checksShort, frequent, with explanations
Case studies / scenariosApplication and analysisUse realistic local contexts
Peer reviewCritical thinking, feedbackProvide a rubric
Interactive videos / H5PActive viewingEmbed questions
Live webinarsReal-time interactionRecord for those who miss them
Reflective journals / e-portfoliosMetacognitionProvide prompts

Synchronous vs. Asynchronous learning

Synchronous Asynchronous
TimeSame time (live)Different times
ExamplesZoom/Meet class, live chatForums, recorded lectures, email
StrengthImmediate interactionFlexibility; time to think
WeaknessTime zone and bandwidth problemsSlower interaction; may feel isolating
Key Point: In low-bandwidth settings such as many parts of Ghana, mix asynchronous and low-data options (PDF notes, audio, WhatsApp) with short live sessions.

4. The Community of Inquiry (Col) Framework

Garrison, Anderson and Archer (2000) explained that meaningful online learning occurs when three kinds of presence overlap:

Presence Meaning How to create it
Cognitive presenceLearners construct meaning through reflection and discourse; move from a trigger event to exploration, integration and resolutionChallenging questions, case problems, reflection tasks
Social presenceLearners project themselves as real people and feel part of a groupIntroductions, ice-breakers, friendly tone, group work, emoticons
Teaching presenceDesign, facilitation and direction of the learning process by the teacherClear course design, active facilitation, timely feedback, announcements
  • The three presences overlap to form the educational experience.
  • Cognitive presence phases (Practical Inquiry model): triggering event -> exploration -> integration -> resolution.
  • Teaching presence has three parts: design and organisation, facilitation of discourse, direct instruction.
Example: In a Moodle forum, the instructor posts a real case (triggering event). Students exchange ideas (exploration), connect them with theory (integration), and propose a solution (resolution). The instructor guides (teaching presence) and the friendly tone builds community (social presence).

5. Best Practices Checklist

  • Provide clear expectations, deadlines and grading criteria.
  • Respond quickly to questions and provide regular feedback.
  • Keep the interface simple and consistent; make it mobile-friendly.
  • Use accessible materials.
  • Build community early.
  • Test the course before launch (check links, quizzes, dates).
  • Use learner data and feedback to improve.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• LMS = platform for delivering, managing and tracking online learning (Moodle, Canvas, Google Classroom).
• Good structure: consistent, chunked, clear, aligned, realistic workload; include an orientation/start-here section.
• Engaging activities: forums, projects, quizzes, case studies, peer review, interactive video.
• Synchronous = same time; asynchronous = different times; mix according to bandwidth and needs.
• Col (Garrison, Anderson, Archer, 2000): cognitive, social and teaching presence.
• Practical inquiry phases: triggering event, exploration, integration, resolution.

Topic 12: Implementation and Management

Week 12 | Managing implementation, teacher training, technical and logistical issues, copyright and fair use

Overview

A good design can fail if it is not implemented well. Implementation is the phase in which the instruction is actually delivered to learners. It involves preparing people (instructors and learners), technology, resources and the environment, and managing everything while the course runs.

Think of opening a new hospital ward: the building and equipment are ready (development), but patients cannot be treated well unless staff are trained, supplies are in place and procedures are managed. The same is true of instruction.

1. What Implementation Involves

  • Preparing and training instructors and facilitators.
  • Preparing learners (orientation and support).
  • Setting up technology and the learning environment.
  • Delivering the instruction.
  • Monitoring progress and solving problems.
  • Collecting data for evaluation.

2. Preparing Instructors (Teacher/Faculty Training)

Even the best materials fail if those who deliver them do not understand or accept them.

  • Offer orientation on objectives, content, methods and the LMS.
  • Provide hands-on practice with tools.
  • Give guides, job aids and tutorials.
  • Provide ongoing support (helpdesk, mentors, communities of practice).
  • Address resistance to change by showing benefits and involving teachers early.
  • Use models such as TPACK (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge): effective teaching needs a combination of technology, pedagogy and content knowledge.
Example: Before launching a new Moodle course, a university trains tutors for two days, gives each a one-page quick guide, and sets up a WhatsApp helpline for the first month.

3. Preparing and Supporting Learners

  • Orientation: how to log in, navigate, submit assignments and communicate.
  • Clear expectations about time, participation and assessment.
  • Support services: technical help, academic advising, counselling.
  • Encourage self-discipline and time management for distance learners.
Area Issues to manage
InfrastructureReliable internet, electricity and backup power; computer labs; server capacity
Devices and softwareCompatibility, licences, updates; mobile access; offline options
Data and securityUser accounts, passwords, privacy, protection of learner data, backups
Technical supportHelpdesk, troubleshooting guides, response time
Scheduling and logisticsTimetables, venues, registration, distribution of materials, deadlines
Budget and resourcesCosts of data, hardware, training, maintenance
Contingency plansWhat to do if the system fails (alternative materials, extended deadlines)
  • Early warning: monitor participation and contact learners who fall behind.

4. Technical and Logistical Considerations

Project management basics

  • Define roles and responsibilities.
  • Use a timeline (Gantt chart) and milestones.
  • Communicate regularly with stakeholders.
  • Manage risks (identify, rank, plan responses).
  • Document processes and decisions.
  • Pilot before full launch (a "soft launch").
Key Point: Always run a pilot with a small group first. It exposes problems cheaply before full implementation.

5. Copyright and Fair Use

Definition: Copyright is the legal right of creators to control the use and copying of their original works (text, images, music, video, software). Fair use (in some countries, "fair dealing") allows limited use of copyrighted material without permission for purposes such as teaching, criticism, research and scholarship.
  • In Ghana, copyright is governed by the Copyright Act, 2005 (Act 690), which also provides for limited exceptions for education. Laws differ between countries, so always check local rules and institutional policies.
  • Fair use usually considers: the purpose of use (educational and non-commercial is favoured), the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market for the original.
  • Using small portions with proper citation for class teaching is more likely acceptable than copying a whole textbook and sharing it.

Ways to stay safe

  • Cite and attribute sources.
  • Use Open Educational Resources (OER).
  • Use Creative Commons (CC) licences correctly: CC BY (attribution), CC BY-SA (share alike), CC BY-NC (non-commercial), CC BY-ND (no derivatives).
  • Use public domain works.
  • Seek permission or licences when in doubt.
  • Avoid plagiarism: presenting someone else's work as your own; use plagiarism checkers and teach academic integrity.
Term Meaning
Public domainWorks whose copyright has expired or never existed; free to use
Creative CommonsStandard licences that allow sharing under stated conditions
OERTeaching and learning materials freely available for use and adaptation
Fair use / fair dealingLimited use of copyrighted work without permission for purposes like education

6. Privacy and Ethics

  • Protect learners' personal data and grades.
  • Obtain consent before recording or sharing learner work.
  • Follow data protection laws (for Ghana, the Data Protection Act, 2012, Act 843).
  • Be fair and inclusive in access to technology.
What I Must Remember for the Exam

• Implementation = delivery + preparation of instructors, learners, technology and environment.
• Train instructors (orientation, hands-on practice, guides, ongoing support); TPACK = technology + pedagogy + content.
• Support learners with orientation, helpdesk, early warning.
• Manage infrastructure, devices, data security, support, scheduling, budget, contingency; run a pilot first.
• Copyright: creators' legal rights; fair use: limited educational use without permission; Ghana: Copyright Act 2005 (Act 690).
• Use OER, public domain, Creative Commons (CC BY etc.), cite sources, avoid plagiarism.
• Protect privacy; data protection (Ghana Act 843, 2012).
